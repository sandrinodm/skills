# Company and Product Naming

## Purpose

Generate strong, scalable company and product names through a professional naming workflow. The goal is not to produce a random list of clever words. The goal is to produce names that are strategically correct, distinctive in-category, easy enough to process and remember, plausible in real market context, flexible enough to grow, and worth sending to trademark counsel and native-language review.

Use this skill for startups choosing a company name, teams launching a product or sub-brand, companies considering a rename, and AI-assisted exploration of naming territories and candidate lists.

## Non-Goals

Do not:
- pick names based only on founder taste
- optimize for "safe" or "comfortable"
- over-index on exact-match `.com` availability
- assume consumers should vote on the final winner
- treat naming as a one-step brainstorming exercise
- present a shortlist without rationale, risks, and usage context

## Inputs

When starting a naming assignment, gather or infer the following. If information is missing, proceed with explicit assumptions and mark them.

Brand fundamentals:
- what is being named: company, product, platform, feature, or naming system
- category and adjacent categories
- core offering
- primary audience
- secondary audience
- what is truly differentiated
- what must remain true in 5+ years
- what can change over time

Business constraints:
- geographic markets
- languages that matter now
- languages that may matter later
- regulation level
- naming architecture constraints
- whether a descriptive, suggestive, arbitrary, coined, or compound style is preferred
- whether the name must sit well beside an existing parent brand

Strategic goals:
- what the name should do on first impression
- what the name should not imply
- how formal or informal it should feel
- how premium, technical, playful, bold, or human it should feel
- whether the goal is credibility, disruption, warmth, speed, intelligence, trust, or another signal

Practical constraints:
- length preference
- syllable preference
- must-use words or fragments
- must-avoid words or sounds
- competitors to avoid sounding like
- handle or domain flexibility tolerance, when relevant

## Operating Principle

A strong name usually balances three tensions:
1. It is distinctive enough to cut through sameness.
2. It is fluent enough to be processed quickly.
3. It is relevant enough to feel believable.

Avoid extremes:
- too literal: forgettable, weak, or hard to own
- too strange: hard to process, explain, or trust

## Process

### Phase 1: Frame the Assignment

Write a concise naming brief with these headings:
- What are we naming?
- What are we really competing against?
- What has to be true in the future?
- How do competitors sound?
- What should this name make people assume?
- What would be a naming mistake?
- What did the user say is wrong today? (A missing .com, a name that feels cheap, a clash with a competitor.) Treat it as a hard requirement: the recommendation has to fix it, and the finalists get checked against it.

Then write:
- `Win definition`
- `What we have to win`
- `What we need to win`
- `What we need the name to say`

This is the strategic spine of the project.

### Phase 2: Map the Category

Build a fast landscape:
- direct competitors
- indirect competitors
- naming patterns in the category
- overused morphemes, suffixes, prefixes, themes, and metaphors
- category cliches
- whitespace opportunities

Output:
- `What the category overuses`
- `Where we can safely zig`
- `What we must avoid sounding like`

### Phase 3: Choose Exploration Territories

Generate 8 to 15 distinct naming territories. Each territory should be a different source of language, not a slight variation of the same idea.

Use territory types such as direct benefit, emotional outcome, metaphor, motion or physics, nature, tools and craft, mythology, geography, science, systems and infrastructure, speed and movement, resilience and trust, lightness or simplicity, transformation, signals and intelligence, time, texture, and sound.

For each territory, write:
- territory title
- why it fits
- what it signals
- likely risks
- good structural choices for that territory

### Phase 4: Generate at Volume

Generate lots of raw material before judging.

Use multiple constructions:
- real words
- unexpected metaphor words
- suggestive names
- lightly coined names
- compounds
- truncated words
- hybrids
- non-English roots that remain pronounceable for the target audience

Do not stop at 20 names. Start with a raw pool of at least:
- 150 candidates for a founder-grade sprint
- 300+ candidates for a serious naming project
- the number the user asked for, when they asked for a quick sprint (for example 30 to 50)

Generate from this brief's territories, not from a stock naming vocabulary. Words that would fit any company (Aplomb, Nova, Apex, Vertex, Lumen and the like) are the AI default, not a choice: keep one only if it earns its place in a territory, and say why it fits this brief better than another. If a name would work equally well for an accounting tool and a DevOps tool, it isn't doing any work.

For each territory, produce names in different structures.

### Phase 5: Apply Phonetic Intent

Assess whether the sounds fit the desired brand behavior.

Use broad phonetic heuristics:
- front vowels and lighter or fricative sounds can feel lighter, quicker, cleaner, smaller, or sharper
- back vowels and stop or voiced sounds can feel stronger, heavier, more forceful, or more substantial
- plosives can add punch or precision
- sibilants and fricatives can add smoothness, sleekness, or air
- repeated sounds, alliteration, and symmetry can improve memorability
- short, pronounceable names often travel better

Do not use sound symbolism mechanically. Use it to refine fit, not to justify fantasy.

### Phase 6: Separate Creation from Judgment

During generation:
- do not kill names early
- do not say "that will never work"
- do not optimize for legal first
- do not ask "do we like it?"

Instead ask:
- What could this do for us?
- What would this make a competitor feel?
- What message does this unlock?
- Is there a better version of this shape?

Judgment begins only after a large pool exists.

### Phase 7: Score Candidates

Score every serious candidate on a 1-5 scale for:
- Strategic fit
- Distinctiveness in category
- Ease of pronunciation
- Ease of spelling
- Memorability
- Emotional charge
- Believability
- Future stretch
- Trademark promise
- Cross-language safety
- Portfolio fit
- Tagline/story support

Suggested weighting:
- Strategic fit: high
- Distinctiveness: high
- Memorability: high
- Believability: high
- Trademark promise: high
- Cross-language safety: medium/high
- Everything else: medium

Kill candidates that fail hard on:
- obvious descriptiveness in a crowded field
- confusion with direct competitors
- likely negative slang
- ugly pronunciation traps
- narrowness that limits future business expansion

### Phase 8: Build a Shortlist

Create three layers:
- `Raw pool`
- `Working shortlist`
- `Final shortlist`

The final shortlist should usually contain 10 to 20 serious candidates, grouped by territory or structural type. Each candidate needs a one-line rationale and a plain-English explanation of what it helps communicate.

### Phase 9: Test in Context

Never present names naked.

For each finalist, show:
- homepage hero headline
- product card or app label
- conference booth line
- investor slide
- recruiter pitch
- social bio line
- packaging or UI label, if relevant
- one-sentence positioning line

Then ask:
- Does this feel believable?
- Does it improve the story?
- Does it help the product feel easier to understand?
- Does it create intrigue without confusion?
- Would this be stronger than a literal category label?

Before anything becomes a finalist, run a quick conflict check, because a name you haven't checked can't be recommended:
- Search the web for the name plus the category: same-name companies, products, features in competing tools, and well-known open-source projects in the audience's world (a developer audience knows Cadence as Uber's workflow engine).
- Listen to it: what does it sound like spoken aloud in English and in the target languages, and what can it be misheard as? ("Stoker" is a short hop from "stalker", which hurts a feature that reads everyone's work.)
- Check the exact-name domains for every finalist, even when the user didn't mention domains: `python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_domains.py Name1 Name2 --tlds com,ai,<country>` (the main market's country domain, such as `nl` or `be`). See "Domain Checks" below.
- A taken domain is where same-name companies show up. Open it (`curl -sIL https://name.com | head -20`, or a web search for the domain) and say whether it's a live company in or near the category, a parked page, or listed for sale.
- When the user raised domains, also check one or two realistic alternatives (`getname.com`, `name.app`) and say for each finalist whether it meets the requirement, and at what price.
Drop names that fail, or keep them with the risk stated plainly. Say what you checked and that it isn't legal clearance.

### Phase 10: Make the Recommendation

Recommend:
- 3 top picks
- 2 backup picks
- 1 wild card with the highest upside if the team is willing to be braver

For each recommendation include:
- name
- style category
- pronunciation
- why it works
- likely objections
- strongest use case
- biggest risk
- what copy or design must do to support it

## Domain Checks

`scripts/check_domains.py` checks availability and price for many names at once. For the TLDs Vercel's registrar sells (`.com`, `.ai`, `.app`, `.io`, `.dev` and about 550 more), it uses Vercel's registrar API (https://vercel.com/docs/domains/registrar-api): free, no account, up to 200 domains per request, with prices in USD and a premium flag. Some TLDs are only sold for a minimum term (`.ai`: 2 years), so the script shows prices per year next to the term and what it costs up front. Vercel reports TLDs it doesn't sell (`.nl`, `.be`, `.de`, `.eu`, `.co.uk`, ...) as unavailable whether or not anyone owns them, so the script asks those registries directly (RDAP, then DNS, then whois) and reports registered or not registered, without a price. Never read a Vercel "unavailable" for those TLDs as taken.

```bash
python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_domains.py Stevin Paceline --tlds com,ai,nl,be
python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/check_domains.py --file raw-pool.txt --tlds com --json > domains.json
```

Statuses: `available` (registrable now at the quoted price), `premium` (registrable at a premium price: quote it, it's often hundreds or thousands), `taken` or `registered` (someone holds it), `not registered` (free at a registry Vercel doesn't sell), `unknown` (no answer: say so).

When the user named domains as a requirement, screen the whole raw pool's `.com` in one run before scoring, so domain fit is a scoring input rather than an afterthought. Otherwise check the shortlist. Quote prices per year as the script shows them, mention a minimum term when there is one (".ai: $80 a year, sold as 2 years for $160"), and say that a domain's status can change before it's bought.

## Naming Style Guidance

Descriptive:
- Use when there is low marketing budget, short lifecycle, low need for trademark strength, or clarity matters more than long-term distinction.
- Avoid for venture-scale company naming when differentiation matters.

Suggestive:
- Treat as the default recommendation for most serious projects.
- Use when the team wants clearer meaning than arbitrary names, stronger ownability than descriptive names, and room for storytelling and positioning.

Arbitrary or coined:
- Use when trademark ambition is high, category clutter is large, long-term brand building matters, and the company can support narrative and education.
- Prefer lightly coined names over random nonsense.

Compound:
- Use when two useful meanings create richer mental imagery, better internal memorability, or a layered brand story.

Acronyms:
- Usually avoid unless the expanded phrase matters, the acronym is truly pronounceable and useful, and there is a clear audience reason to abbreviate.

## Heuristics

Favor names that are:
- distinct from category cliches
- easy to say after seeing once
- easy to hear and spell back
- meaningfully open-ended
- strong when spoken aloud
- strong in headlines
- good in a sentence
- capable of supporting a story

Favor names that make people think:
- "That feels new."
- "I think I get where this is going."
- "That sounds like a real company/product."
- "That is more interesting than the alternatives."

Avoid names that are:
- generic category labels
- trend-chasing suffixes used by everyone else
- forced latinizations with no tonal reason
- clever in a way that hurts recall
- legally fragile on day one
- hard to spell after hearing
- too narrow for future expansion
- too dependent on one current feature or technology wave

## AI Guidance

Use AI for territory expansion, metaphor mining, root and morpheme exploration, construction variants, clustering and de-duplication, fast first-pass scoring, and contextual mockups.

Do not use AI alone for final selection, trademark clearance, cross-language clearance, leadership alignment, or emotionally nuanced judgment.

## Standard Deliverable Format

### Section A: Brief

Include:
- naming goal
- audience
- future stretch
- constraints
- category map
- whitespace

### Section B: Territories

Include 8 to 15 territories with rationale.

### Section C: Raw Generation

Include 150 to 300+ candidates separated by territory, unless the user explicitly asks for a smaller sprint.

### Section D: Scored Shortlist

Include 20 candidates with scores.

### Section E: Final Recommendations

Include:
- top 3
- backup 2
- wild card 1

### Section F: Next-Step Checks

Include:
- trademark counsel review
- domain and handle review
- native-speaker linguistic review
- in-context stakeholder review

## Output Behavior

When using this skill, always:
1. Open with the answer in a few lines: the top pick, the safest alternative and the boldest one, each with one sentence of why. Then show the work.
2. Write down assumptions.
3. Show the naming logic.
4. Produce breadth before narrowing.
5. Explain tradeoffs.
6. Distinguish best safe option from highest-upside option.
7. Close with the full recommendation, not just a list.

Scale the deliverable to the request. A quick sprint or a feature name gets a compact answer of roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words: a short brief, the candidates grouped by territory, a ranked shortlist of 5 to 8 with one line each, and the top 3 in context. Skip the 12-criterion score table there. Keep the full A to F report for company and product naming projects.
